"How does one know if they have spotted Mars for sure?"

Mars is often brighter than the other stars and may appear a bit orange, depending on how good your color perception is. With binoculars or a telescope, it will be much bigger than just a dot. Stars look like dots even in a telescope. If you have a good landmark like the Moon nearby, a sky chart and some sense of direction should be sufficient to identify Mars.

Anything close to the zenith is pretty easy to identify. You just look straight up and try to grab something. Away from the zenith, you can figure out the horizontal direction (NESW) and estimate the altitude angle (declination) either by eyeballing or using your outstretched fist. Each fist is about 10 degrees. Armed with this info, you look at the starchart and try to make an educated guess.

According to Sky & Telescope if the clouds weren’t out, tonight I would be able to see Orion above my house, but alas, they are out. I have done a little research on Orion and hope you all find this information interesting and helpful.

Orion is a constellation often referred to as The Hunter. In wartime Orion is a magnificent constellation, which can be easily found by the three stars that make up Orion’s Belt. These three stars point towards Sirius, the brightest star in the constellation of the Larger Dog, Canis Major, situated SE of Orion. There are ten (10) stars that make up Orion:

Melssa: located at Orion’s head, this star is a blue spectral type O giant star, and is part of the Collinder 69 star cluster. It has an apparent visible magnitude 3.39 and is one of the most luminous stars known;

Betelgeuse: located at his right shoulder – this is a red star with a diameter larger than the orbit of Mars. This star is the second brightest in the constellation and the ninth brightest in the night sky. Betelgeuse is actually pronounced “beetle juice”;

Bellatrix: located at his left shoulder is the third brightest star in the constellation, and the 27th star in the night sky. Historically this star was used as a luminosity standard that astronomers could compare with other stars to check for variability. However, it was later discovered that Bellatrix itself is an eruptive variable, changing in luminosity by a few percent over time. It ranges in magnitude from 1.59 to 1.64.It is 240 light years from Earth;

Alnitak, Alnllam, and Mintaka: these three stars make up the asterism commonly known as Orion’s belt: From these three bright stars, one can recognize Orion;

Eta Orionis: located between Delta Orionis and Rigel;

Saiph: located at Orion’s right knee;

Rigel: located at Orion’s right knee, this is a bright blue-white star and it is among the brightest in the sky, it has three companions that are invisible to the naked eye. Rigel is the brightest star in the constellation and the seventh brightest in the night sky. It also has the alternative traditional names Algebar or Elgebar, but these are almost never used; and,

Hatsya: located at the tip of Orion’s sword.

July 1, 2008: Altair is the brightest star of Aquila, the eagle, and one vertex of the Summer Triangle. Altair has almost twice the mass of our Sun and is roughly twice as big in diameter. Altair also spins a lot faster than our Sun. It rotates once every 10 hours, while our Sun takes about a month to complete a rotation. A point at its equator would move at about 130 miles per second -- fast enough to cross the U.S. in less than half a minute. One reason that Altair spins so fast is that it's much younger than the Sun. The Sun probably turned much faster when it was young, but its magnetic field acts as a brake, slowing its rotation. Altair forms the lowest vertex of the Summer Triangle, which stands high overhead at nightfall. The highest and brightest vertex is the star Vega, very near the zenith, while Deneb forms the triangle's easternmost point. Altair lies roughly at the midpoint of the line formed by Jupiter (the brightest starlike object in the night sky), Altair, and Deneb, a line which points up and to the east.

Venus has adorned the southwestern twilight sky since late August. No other star or planet can come close to matching Venus in brilliance. During World War II, aircraft spotters sometimes mistook Venus for an enemy airplane. There were even cases in which Venus drew anti-aircraft fire. Though not as bright as Venus, Jupiter is also quite brilliant, and over the next couple of days, we'll have a chance to see these two shiny objects right next to each other. Sometimes, such an occasion brings with it a sudden spike of phone calls to local planetariums, weather offices and even police precincts. Fall/Winter (12/1/11):

The constellation Perseus can be seen in the fall and winter. The constellations brightest star is Alpha Persei. The Alpha Persei is a cluster that is 50 million years old. The most interesting star in the Perseus constellaton is Algol. Algol is 75 light years from Earth and is an eclipsing binary star. About every 3 days the fainter star passes in front of the brighter star making the Algol look dimmer.

Canis Minor and Procyon
by Kitelinger, Jana at 1/14/2014 | 11:50 PM
 I continue to be disappointed by the cloudy skies.  I borrowed my Grandpa's binoculars for this class and have yet to be able to use them.  
As I retreated yet again to SkyGazer, I noticed a constellation that I thought was a little silly.  How could anyone ever see a creative object in a constellation that consisted of two stars with a single line drawn between them?  I became intrigued and decided that this single line of a constellation would be my Stargazing research for the day (night?).  After consulting my star and planet locator spinny-dial thing (I like to pretend SkyGazer is the real sky, even if I'm only fooling myself) it appeared that I had discovered the constellation of Canis Minor.  I noticed that one of the two stars that made up this line of a constellation was one of the brightest stars in the sky.  I like bright and shiny things, so I decided to read a bit more about this star, Procyon.  It turns out that the reason it's so bright is not because of it's intrinsic luminosity, but rather because it's simply so close to our own sun.  It's the 14th closest star system from our sun, to be exact, at 11.4 light years away.  It's name comes from Greek and means "before the dog," referencing the fact that it rises before Sirius (and the rest of Canis Major) in the sky each night.
